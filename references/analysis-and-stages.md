# Chat analysis and observable relationship stages

Read this reference when the user asks what a message means, whether interest is mutual, where the relationship stands, or what the next move should test.

## Analyze an exchange in four passes

### 1. Reconstruct the record

Identify speaker, order, timing only when known, explicit questions, commitments, refusals, and changes of topic. Quote or tightly paraphrase the minimum useful fragments. Do not infer tone from punctuation alone.

### 2. Identify conversational function

A message may primarily be sharing, asking, acknowledging, joking, testing logistics, setting a boundary, repairing, closing, or merely maintaining contact. Prefer the simplest function supported by the text.

### 3. Compare explanations

Use a small hypothesis set rather than one dramatic story. Typical competing explanations include:

- interest versus ordinary friendliness;
- temporary busyness versus reduced interest;
- awkwardness versus avoidance;
- playful ambiguity versus reluctance to be direct;
- preference for a slower pace versus a boundary against progression.

State what future observation would distinguish the leading explanations.

### 4. Choose a low-regret next move

The next move should either improve the interaction or reveal useful information without imposing high pressure. Prefer one step: respond to the content, offer a specific low-pressure plan, ask one clarifying question, reduce intensity, or leave space.

## Evidence hierarchy

Use this order when signals conflict:

1. **Explicit communication**: direct interest, refusal, boundary, or relationship discussion.
2. **Concrete behavior**: accepting a plan, proposing an alternative, following through, repeated voluntary initiative.
3. **Repeated interaction pattern**: balanced questions, remembered details, sustained personal disclosure, reliable repair after friction.
4. **Single conversational cue**: warmth, teasing, compliments, length, emojis, quick replies.
5. **Ambiguous metadata**: read receipts, online status, posting while not replying, platform likes.

Negative evidence follows the same principle. A clear "no" overrides flirtatious ambiguity. Repeated declined invitations without alternatives is stronger evidence than one delayed answer. Silence has many causes, but repeated one-sided pursuit is evidence about reciprocity even when the cause is unknown.

## Stage model

Use stages as descriptive bands, not points earned or a guaranteed ladder.

### S0 — No established interaction

There is a match, introduction, follow, or isolated contact, but not enough mutual exchange to infer continuity.

### S1 — Basic contact

Both can exchange ordinary messages, but continuity and personal interest remain uncertain. Most content is situational or polite.

### S2 — Reciprocal familiarity

There is a repeatable two-way conversation: both contribute, ask some follow-ups, remember details, or share voluntarily. This supports comfort, not necessarily romantic interest.

### S3 — Selective investment

The other person repeatedly spends discretionary attention, initiates, deepens topics, makes room in their schedule, or chooses this connection beyond mere politeness. Romantic meaning is possible but still needs corroboration.

### S4 — Mutual romantic exploration

Romantic or flirtatious framing is reciprocated across multiple interactions, or both are actively testing dates and compatibility. Playfulness alone is insufficient; look for voluntary investment and action.

### S5 — Explicit dating or relationship

Both have acknowledged dating intent, established recurring dates, discussed exclusivity or expectations, or otherwise made the relationship explicit. Do not infer exclusivity from frequency alone.

## Derive the stage from dimensions

Assess each dimension as absent, emerging, or established:

- **continuity** — the interaction reliably resumes;
- **reciprocity** — initiative, questions, disclosure, and effort are not persistently one-sided;
- **personal depth** — there is increasing knowledge, trust, and individual specificity;
- **romantic framing** — attraction or specialness is expressed and reciprocated;
- **action** — plans become concrete and are followed through;
- **boundary safety** — pace and limits are respected by both sides.

Choose the highest stage supported by a pattern across relevant dimensions. When one dimension is far ahead of the rest, report a mixed state such as "S3 investment, but only S2 action evidence." If evidence is sparse or contradictory, choose the lower defensible stage and state what is missing.

## Common interpretation errors

- **Single-signal inflation**: treating a long reply, fast reply, or compliment as proof of attraction.
- **Outcome bias**: assuming no reply proves the previous message was bad.
- **Confirmation bias**: counting warm signals and explaining away every cool signal.
- **Frequency illusion**: mistaking many low-substance messages for increasing closeness.
- **Fantasy attribution**: assigning motives that were never stated and cannot be tested.
- **Escalation fallacy**: assuming more intensity will fix low reciprocity.
